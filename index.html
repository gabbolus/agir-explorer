Age Group Income Ratio Explorer

How does the income of people aged 55–64 compare with that of people aged 25–34? Explore 37 countries from 2004 to 2024, and see which sources of income drove the change.

Based on Guaitoli, G. and Pancrazi, R. (2026), “Age-Income Gaps: The Role of Skill Congestion”, European Economic Review, doi:10.1016/j.euroecorev.2026.105408. The dashboard extends the paper’s data beyond 2018.

How to read this dashboard

AGIR = average income of people agedaverage income, 55–64 average income of people agedaverage income, 25–34

Both are averages over everyone in the age group, measured in the same year.

AGIR is the Age Group Income Ratio. An AGIR of 1.20 means that today’s 55–64 year-olds earn, on average, 20% more than today’s 25–34 year-olds.

✓ What it shows

A snapshot of how income is shared between two generations alive at the same time. A rising AGIR means income is becoming more concentrated among older people; a falling one, among younger people.

✕ What it does not show

  • Not “the young are getting poorer”. The ratio can rise while the young’s income also rises, if the old’s rises faster.
  • Not an age-income profile. Today’s 55–64 year-olds are not today’s 25–34 year-olds grown older, so the ratio does not say how a person’s income grows with age.

What is measured. All people in each age group, working or not. Income is labour income plus pensions and other transfers, net of income taxes. Capital income and wealth are not observed, so the gap is likely understated in high-income countries (a lower bound). Changes in surveys can also move the numbers; countries affected are marked †.

Age Group Income Ratio over time

    What the line says

    What drove the change?

    GRD = 55–64 income growth − 25–34 income growth

    Income growth is the change in average income between the two chosen periods, as a percentage of initial income, per year. GRD stands for Growth Rate Differential and is measured in percentage points per year.

    Income growth depends on the change in wages and employment, plus changes in transfer income per recipient and the share of transfer recipients.

    A positive GRD means the gap in favour of the old is widening (the Age Group Income Ratio rises by roughly that many per cent per year); a negative one means it is narrowing. The dark marker in the chart is the total GRD. Each coloured bar is one margin’s share of it. Each margin compares the old with the young: positive means the old gains more (or loses less) than the young on that margin; negative means the young do better.

    Wages

    What it is How much the pay of people who have a job changes (net labour income per employed person).

    Positive when employed 55–64 year-olds’ pay grows faster than employed 25–34 year-olds’ pay.

    Negative when the young’s pay grows faster.

    Employment

    What it is How the share of people in work changes, valued at what a worker earned at the start.

    Positive when the share of 55–64 year-olds in work rises faster than the share of 25–34 year-olds (for example, older people retiring later).

    Negative when the young’s employment rate rises faster.

    Transfer income

    What it is How the size of the average transfer (pensions and other benefits) changes for those who receive one.

    Positive when the average benefit of the old grows faster than that of the young.

    Negative when the young’s average benefit grows faster.

    Transfer share

    What it is How the share of people receiving any transfer changes, valued at the average transfer at the start.

    Positive when the share of 55–64 year-olds receiving transfers rises faster (or falls more slowly) than that of 25–34 year-olds.

    Negative when the young’s share rises faster.

    Other income

    What it is Everything not captured by the four margins above, such as labour income of people not classified as employed.

    Positive when this part of the old’s income grows faster than the young’s.

    Negative when the young’s grows faster.

    Data and methods

    Data. Luxembourg Income Study (LIS) Database, all waves available in October 2026, accessed through LISSY. The sample covers 37 countries with individual-level income from 2004 onwards. South Africa is shown with a caveat: its survey changed in 2023.

    Income. Disposable income is personal labour income plus transfers (pensions, unemployment, family and education benefits), net of personal income taxes. Values are in constant prices.

    Waves. 2004–06, 2007–09, …, 2022–24. Each annual survey in a wave gets equal weight. The wave value of the Age Group Income Ratio is the mean of its annual values. Growth between waves is annualised using the average survey year of each wave.

    Decomposition. Average income of an age group is y = e·w + p·Θ + other, where e is the employment rate, w is net labour income of the employed, p is the share receiving transfers and Θ is the transfer received. The change in y splits exactly into e1Δw (wages), w0Δe (employment), p1ΔΘ (transfer income), Θ0Δp (transfer share) and other income, each divided by initial income.

    Groups. Countries are classed as high-income or middle-income by 2004 GDP per capita (PPP), as in the paper. Group lines are simple averages across countries. Shaded bands around the group averages and vertical lines around country points are 95% confidence intervals.

    Imputation in group averages. Not all countries have a survey in every period (for example, 2022–24 data are not yet available for several countries). To keep the set of countries constant, a country with no survey in a period enters the group average with its most recent Age Group Income Ratio (last value carried forward). These averages are therefore partially imputed; they are shown with hollow markers, and the hover text lists the countries concerned. Country lines and the decomposition use observed values only.

    Limitations

    What the numbers can and cannot tell you.

    • Averages, not distributions. The ratio compares the average income of two age groups. It does not show how income is distributed within each group.
    • Capital income and wealth are not observed. The data have no individual-level capital income, and wealth is increasingly concentrated among older people. The ratio therefore likely understates the age-income gap, and its rise, in high-income countries: read it as a lower bound.
    • Individual income only. Income is personal labour income plus personal transfers, net of income taxes. Household-wide benefits (child allowances, housing benefits) are left out; in the paper, adding them gave similar results for 2004–2018.
    • Accounting, not causes. The decomposition shows which income component moved, not why. It also approximates the change in the Age Group Income Ratio well only when growth is small. Explanations such as skill congestion come from the paper’s model, not from this dashboard.
    • Uneven and incomplete coverage. Countries are surveyed in different years and some recent periods are missing. Group averages carry the last available value forward (partially imputed); country lines and the decomposition use observed values only.
    • Breaks and the pandemic. Changes in surveys can move the numbers; see the † notes (for example Mexico 2024 and South Africa 2023). In 2019–21, job-retention schemes are recorded as wages in some countries and as transfers in others, which shifts weight between those two margins.
